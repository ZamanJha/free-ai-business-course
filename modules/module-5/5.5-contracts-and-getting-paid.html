Contracts & Getting Paid

The scope doc and payment terms that keep a small build from turning into unpaid, unbounded work.

Before you start this lesson
  • A verbal yes, or a prospect at the paperwork stage
  • Your handoff decision from 3.4

Scope creep is the most common way a first client project goes from profitable to a headache. A short written scope, agreed before you build, prevents almost all of it.

The scope-of-work template

Scope of work template copy, fill in brackets, send before you start building 
Project: [CLIENT BUSINESS] Custom App

What's included:
- [Feature 1, plain English]
- [Feature 2, plain English]
- [Feature 3, plain English]
- Hosting and a live link
- One round of revisions after the first version is shown

What's not included (available as an add-on):
- [Anything you're deliberately excluding]

Timeline: [X] business days from deposit received
Price: $[AMOUNT], due as [50% deposit / 50% on delivery] or [monthly retainer starting at $X/mo]

By approving this, we agree to the scope above. Anything beyond this list will be scoped and priced separately.

Getting paid

Take a deposit before you start building, even a small one (25–50%). It's not really about the money at that size — it's a real signal the client is committed, which filters out the tire-kickers before you've spent your Module 7 sprint week on someone who was never going to pay.

What you're actually selling, and what you aren't

The single most common mess in this business is a scope that never says who owns and pays for the platform after launch. Settle it in writing, in one short clause, and you avoid the awkward month-five conversation entirely.

There are two clean arrangements. Both work. Mixing them doesn't.

  • You host it. The build lives in your workspace, you maintain it, and they pay you monthly. Price that monthly fee with your own plan cost in mind so it's comfortably profitable — this is the arrangement that turns one client into recurring revenue, and it's usually the right one.
  • They own it. The project is transferred to their account at handoff, they carry the platform cost themselves, and your involvement ends or becomes ad-hoc. Charge more up front for this, because you're giving up the recurring side.

Whichever you pick, write down explicitly what happens to their data if the relationship ends. It costs you nothing to be generous about, and it's the clause that makes a cautious owner comfortable signing.

One thing to avoid: promising indefinite hosting at no cost as a closing sweetener. It feels small when you have one client and becomes a real liability at ten.

What you should have when this lesson is done Your own filled-out scope-of-work doc, ready to send to client #1 before you start building.
