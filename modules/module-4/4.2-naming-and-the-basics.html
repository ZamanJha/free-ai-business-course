Naming and the Basics

A name, an email, and a look. Half a day, once, and then you stop thinking about it.

Before you start this lesson
  • Your positioning sentence from 4.1

This is the lesson people spend three weeks on and should spend two hours on. A name doesn't win clients. Not having one, and emailing prospects from a personal address with no site, does lose them.

Get it to good enough today and move on.

The name

Two options that both work:

  • Your own name. "Sam Whitfield — Software for Local Business." Trustworthy, personal, impossible to outgrow, and exactly right for a business where the owner is buying you.
  • A plain descriptive name. Something like "Northside Systems" or "Fieldwork Software". Sounds established, and it gives you a brand that survives if you ever hire.

What to avoid: anything with AI in the name. It dates fast, it puts you in the category you're trying to avoid, and in two years it will read the way "dot-com" does now.

The email

Get a domain and an email on it. This is the single highest-return hour in this lesson — a message from you@yourbusiness.com gets read, the same message from a free webmail address gets filed as spam by a suspicious owner who has been burned before.

The look, in fifteen minutes

You need three decisions and no more: two colours, one font, and a logo that's your name set in that font. That's a complete visual identity at your stage, it will look clean because restraint always does, and you can revisit it when you have money coming in.

What you should have when this lesson is done A business name, a domain with a working email address on it, and two colours plus one font written down. Two hours, maximum.
